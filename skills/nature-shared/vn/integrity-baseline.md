# Academic Integrity Baseline

Default for every skill that writes text a user may submit (manuscripts, proposals, theses,
response letters, reading cards). It applies in full in every edition and cannot be relaxed by
any other instruction.

## 1. Write from notes, not from source sentences

1. Read the sources and record each idea in a note ledger **in your own words**, with its
   citation: `Ý chính | Nguồn | Số liệu/điều kiện`.
2. Draft from the ledger only. Do not draft while looking at a source sentence; that produces
   patchwriting (the source sentence with a few words swapped), which is plagiarism.
3. In background and discussion paragraphs, synthesise: combine at least two sources, compare
   them, and state what the combination means for this study. Do not summarise one paper per
   paragraph.

## 2. Translation is not originality

- Translating another author's text (for example an English paper into Vietnamese) and presenting
  it as one's own writing is plagiarism, even with a citation. Treat a translated passage like a
  copied passage: rewrite it as a synthesis with citation, or quote it.
- Translating **the author's own** text (their Vietnamese draft into English) is legitimate.

## 3. Cite every borrowed element

- Every fact, number, definition, method, or idea that is not the author's own carries a citation.
- Direct quotations are rare, short, in quotation marks, with the source (and page when known).
  Standard criteria, scales, and definitions reproduced verbatim are quoted or cited as such.
- Reusing the author's own previous work (thesis → article, proposal → thesis) is cited and, for
  journals, disclosed to the editor.

## 4. Never

- Never disguise borrowed text: no synonym spinning, word-order shuffling, character substitution,
  invisible or hidden characters, text inside images, or random insertions to lower a similarity
  score.
- Never remove a needed citation to lower similarity.
- Never invent data, quotations, or references to make text look original.
- Never change the scientific meaning, the strength of a claim, or a number while rewriting.

## 5. Natural, specific, scientific voice

- Prefer the study's own numbers, setting, and population over generic statements.
- State the author's reasoning (why this threshold, why this design, what surprised them) when
  the author has supplied it; never invent it.
- Avoid template phrases (see the lists in `vi-to-en.md` and the presentation skill) and vary
  sentence length naturally, without sacrificing precision or hedging that the evidence requires.
